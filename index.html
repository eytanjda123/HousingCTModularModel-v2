      Input-to-output map ·

      How each input reaches the two gaps

      The model has two gaps, paid by two kinds of money. The sales gap (appraised value minus what the buyer can pay) takes buyer-side money only. The developer gap (total cost minus what the sale brings in) takes developer-side money only. Hover a box to trace it, or click to lock the highlight. Click empty map space to unlock.

        Inter-city comparison

        Same building, three cities

        Each bar is the building's total development cost and who pays for it. Purple segments are buyer-side money, which only fills the sales gap. Blue, teal and amber segments are developer-side money, filled in order: HDF, AHP, HTCC, NMTC (when switched on), then DOH up to its cap. Red is still unfunded. The dashed line is the appraised value, and triangles mark the baseline.

        Funding stack: what the program documents say

        A stacking rule is only built in when a document states it. Everything else is marked for legal review. Unchecking a source takes it out of every city's stack, so you can test a "what if this doesn't come through" case. It is not a finding that the source is unavailable.

        Three appraisal cases · all three cities

        Existing homes, the size-adjusted base case, and the DOH precedent

        The appraisal sets the sale price, and nobody knows yet what a new modular two-family will appraise for. The low case uses the median sale of existing 2-unit homes (CAMA). The base case ($340K) scales the $420K new-build value in the redacted New Haven DOH application down to our smaller home, and matches the 2024–25 resales of new two-families in Hartford ($133–$151 per sq ft). The high case is the $420K precedent itself. A higher value shrinks the developer gap but raises the sales gap, property tax and maintenance reserve. Every other input stays as you've set it. The basis selector on the left sets which case drives the rest of the page.

        Homeowner ·

        Can the buyer sustain ownership?

        The same building and appraised value at four income targets. Costs include taxes, insurance, energy, common utilities and a maintenance reserve. The income left over is compared with the MIT living-wage budget for the selected household. The stress test hits the owner with a 3-month vacancy, a 20% tax reassessment and a $10K repair at once. The highlighted column is the current income target.

        Modular vs. stick-built ·

        Does a bigger order make modular cheaper?

        Total development cost of one building by manufacturer and order size. Each manufacturer's cost falls by its learning rate every time the order doubles. Stick-built is calibrated to the Hartford partner's ~$800K two-family. Manufacturer costs are faculty placeholders, not quotes.

        Add-on features and procurement ·

        What each add-on costs the project and saves the owner

        Every add-on starts switched off, so the base model still matches the workbook. Each row shows what that feature would do on its own, with the rest of your current settings. The project pays the upfront cost, which raises the developer gap. The owner keeps the monthly savings. Turn features on and set their details in the panel on the left, under "Add-on features and procurement".

        Portfolio and mixed income

        A phase of buildings across cities and incomes

        Each row is a group of buildings in one city at one owner income target. The developer gap per building doesn't change with income when a homebuyer subsidy fills the sales gap, but the buyer-side money does. Totals are checked against the caps the program documents state. The starting rows are an example only.

        What changed from version 1

        Built on the feedback-response workbook

        • Two gaps instead of one. Buyer-side money (Time To Own, DOH homebuyer subsidy) only fills the sales gap. Developer-side money (HDF, AHP, HTCC, DOH developer subsidy) only fills the developer gap. This follows the reviewer's note that buyer subsidies can't fill the developer gap.
        • Line-item pro forma. Percentage loads are replaced by site work, itemized soft costs from the redacted course budgets, a Phase I, construction-loan interest from the schedule, land, and a developer fee kept inside total cost.
        • Manufacturer and order volume. Four modular manufacturers, each with a learning curve, plus stick-built recalibrated to ~$800K.
        • 80% AMI base case, following the consensus in the room at External Review 1.
        • Appraisal basis is a choice: the existing 2-unit median ($300K), a size-adjusted new-build value ($340K, the base case since Oct 28), or the $420K DOH new-build precedent.
        • Homeowner costs now include energy bills and a maintenance reserve at 1% of value, plus a residual-income check against the MIT living wage and a stress test.
        • Mill rates are updated to FY2027 (Hartford 69.95, New Haven 39.96, Waterbury 43.34). The workbook uses 39.75 for New Haven.
        • Add-on features and procurement (new, off by default): rooftop solar, battery storage, an efficiency package, two custom slots, other hard-cost line items, and a warehouse for bulk buying.
        • Base-case appraisal $340K (Oct 28): size-adjusted from the $420K DOH precedent and checked against new two-family sales in the state's property records. $300K and $420K are shown as the low and high cases.
        • Oct 28 update: New Markets Tax Credits added to the developer-side stack (off by default); the appraisal shown as two cases side by side; a choice of assessed value for property tax; sourced defaults for the mortgage rate (CHFA), utility allowances, owner energy, water and sewer (Housing Authority of New Haven), insurance by city (NerdWallet) and the construction-loan rate (prime + 1%).
        • Hartford and Waterbury use New Haven's itemized site and soft costs, which are flagged, with their own AMI, rents, tax rate and sales comps.
